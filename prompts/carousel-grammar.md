# Carousel frame grammar

Loaded only when the format is `carousel`. The template is the product: the
topics change every week for years, the frames should not change at all. Ten
consecutive covers, seen together in a profile grid, must read as one account.

## The frames

**Frame 1 — cover.** A claim, four to seven words, no subject named. No time
promise ("in 5 minutes"), no topic label, no question. It states the stakes.
Nothing else on the frame competes with it.

**Frames 2 to 6 — value frames.** Three at minimum, five at most. Each one:

- a numbered eyebrow, so the reader always knows where they are
- a headline that is a claim, not a heading
- one or two lines of body, never three — if it needs three, it is two frames
- **one proof object** (see below)
- a closing line naming the consequence

**Frame 7 — application.** How to do it: numbered steps, or a decision
checklist with the pass condition stated. This is the frame that earns the
save, so it must be genuinely usable by someone who does not know you.

**Frame 8 — steal these.** Three to five quoted lines the reader could say
out loud in a meeting on Monday. Not a summary, not "follow for more". The
final line carries the named principle. Point to the blog for the long
version, once, plainly.

Six to eight frames total.

## The proof object rule

**Every value frame carries one visual object. Never text alone.** This is
the highest-value rule here and the easiest to skip. A frame of well-set
type is an opinion; a frame with an object is evidence.

Permitted:

- a before-and-after table (messaging, positioning, statement copy)
- a timeline strip (how a crisis window actually unfolds, hour by hour)
- a two-column comparison (what the board asks vs what marketing prepared)
- a simple chart, one series, no legend
- an annotated quote from a public filing or public statement
- a framework diagram, four nodes maximum

Forbidden, without exception: stock photography, invented dashboards,
invented screenshots of any real company's systems, anything a reader could
mistake for a genuine record. A fabricated proof object is worse than no
proof object — it is the one mistake that cannot be walked back.

**Sourcing.** Objects are built from public material, or from generalised
patterns. Never from any employer's, client's or partner's internal data. If
the only version of an object that would work requires private information,
the frame gets a different object or the post gets a different angle.

## The utility test

Before the deck is finished, answer this in one sentence: **what does the
reader now have that they did not have before?**

A checklist. A set of questions. A threshold. A decision tree. A sequence.

If the honest answer is "a point of view", the deck is not finished. Points
of view belong in Reels. Carousels are where things people keep live, and
saves and sends are the whole reason this format is on the calendar.

## Copy rules specific to this format

Headlines are claims, never topics. "Boards do not read press releases"
beats "Crisis communication tips".

No frame is ever labelled Conclusion, Summary, Recap or Takeaways.

Plain sentences. No markdown, no bullet characters, no emoji — the template
supplies every piece of styling, and stray characters break the render.
