# Hooks that earn their promise

The assigned blog determines the subject. Find its most useful specific tool,
decision, example or disagreement before writing the hook. Do not recycle a
sentence from instructions, the brand exemplars or past posts.

## Cover, opening and payoff

Name a recognisable situation on the cover and leave a clear question to answer.
A specific question is welcome; a mysterious label is not. The hook sharpens
the same promise. Start delivering the answer in the second beat or slide.
Delay a company name only if useful; never hide the subject itself.

Compare three possible openings internally. Choose on clarity, relevance and
whether the visible content actually delivers, not on alarm or clever phrasing.
Use only source-supported quantities and personal experience.

## Something the reader can use

Every format needs a usable artifact: a copyable prompt, a template with fields,
a checklist with concrete actions, a worked example, or a test with a stated
decision rule. Show it on screen. Music-backed Reels have no spoken narration;
voiceover fields cannot supply information missing from the visible frames.

Preserve the article's specific mechanism. Do not turn different articles into
the same generic advice about trust, approvals, procurement or budgets. A new
title over the same argument does not count as a new post. A closing principle
may explain the tool, but cannot replace it.

## Final checks

- The cover names the situation; the hook and body answer the same question.
- A viewer can use the artifact without visiting the blog or hearing narration.
- The practical decision differs from recently published, approved and queued work.
- All promised items are present, and all claims keep their source's meaning.
- Opening lengths and exact-text requirements follow the output schema.
- Low reach and a handful of saves do not establish a winning hook or topic.
